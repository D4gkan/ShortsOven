# "Found This On The Internet" Post — Idea-Engineering Framework

This is a reusable engine, not a template with content in it. Every run
invents its own fact, artifact, accounts, and comment from scratch. Nothing
below should be copied into an output — it's the machine that builds it.

## 1. Pick the emotional target first

Before anything else, pick the ONE feeling this post should leave someone
with. This choice drives every downstream decision — mechanism, voice,
artifact, pacing. Rotate across these; don't default to the same one every
time, or the output starts feeling like a template instead of a real find:

- **Funny / absurd** — the reader laughs or sends it to someone immediately.
- **Petty outrage / relatable exasperation** — "this is so real it's annoying."
- **Uncanny / unsettling** — something quietly wrong that lingers.
- **Jaw-dropping disbelief** — "there's no way this is real."
- **Wholesome subversion** — braces for something bad, gets something sweet
  (or the reverse — braces for wholesome, gets a dark turn).

A post that's merely "mildly interesting" won't move. Pick a target strong
enough that someone would tag a friend or reply within three seconds of
reading it.

The story itself should be weird, funny, shocking, chaotic, lively, and
exciting. Boring, flat, or slowly-building stories are not allowed. The core
story should feel like a real person is urgently telling the most unbelievable
thing they saw, heard, or found online, with a strong sense of momentum and
surprise.

Favor stories that feel genuinely alarming, uncanny, darkly funny, or deeply
strange. The best versions hit with a sharp mix of disbelief, dread, and
comic timing. The story should not be safe or polite; it should feel like the
kind of thing that makes someone stop scrolling and immediately send it to a
friend.

Allowed tone includes dry, suggestive, teasing innuendo and playful sexual
humor when it is indirect, not explicit, and not graphic. Keep it implied,
clever, and wink-and-nudge rather than explicit or pornographic.

## 2. The underlying mechanism

Every version of this format runs on an asymmetry, stated plainly, then
reframed. Pick ONE, chosen to serve the emotional target from step 1:

- **Magnitude mismatch** — a small input maps to a wildly disproportionate
  output, or something that sounds huge turns out nearly invisible.
- **Escalation** — a sequence that starts ordinary and, step by step, tips
  into absurd. The reveal is the trajectory, not one fact.
- **Claim vs. artifact** — an unbelievable statement, followed by something
  that looks like proof.
- **Deadpan reframe** — something that reads as neutral until a second party
  points out its funny or uncomfortable implication.
- **Mundane container, extraordinary content** — boring packaging undersells
  something huge inside it.

## 3. Comedy craft (apply whenever the target is funny/absurd/outrage)

Humor has its own mechanics, separate from surprise. When comedy is the
target, follow these or the "joke" will just read as a fact with a laugh
emoji attached:

- **Never explain the joke.** The reaction/comment should never state what's
  funny about the setup — it should just add a new, deadpan detail that makes
  the reader do the connecting themselves. Explaining kills it every time.
- **Specificity is the punchline.** An oddly precise, slightly unnecessary
  detail is inherently funnier than a vague one. When inventing content,
  always reach for the more specific, weirder-but-plausible version of any
  number, name, or noun.
- **Understatement beats escalation in the punchline.** The setup can
  escalate; the reaction/final line almost never should — a flat, dry final
  beat lands harder than an exclamation point.
- **Dark humor is allowed when it fits the story.** A comment can land with a
  dry, darkly funny undercut, a sense of dread, or a bitter little twist as
  long as it still feels human and not mean-spirited or forced.
- **Comments should carry a dark, sarcastic edge.** Reactions and replies
  should often feel dry, cutting, or wry — a little cynical, quietly mean,
  or underhandedly funny rather than warm or wholesome. The humor can be
  sarcastic, deadpan, or bleakly amused, but it must still read like a real
  person online rather than a polished punchline.
- **Incongruity, not absurdity for its own sake.** The funniest pairing is
  two believable things that shouldn't belong in the same sentence, not one
  outlandish invented thing.
- **Let the reader be the one who "gets it."** If the joke requires the post
  to point at itself, cut it and find a version that doesn't.

## 3A. Visual comedy engineering

When the emotional target is funny / absurd, the IMAGE itself must contribute
to the joke. Do not treat the artifact as merely evidence for the written
story. The visual should contain a funny, awkward, oddly specific, or
unexpected element that makes the viewer laugh even before fully processing
the text.

Use ONE primary visual-comedy mechanism per generation, chosen naturally:

- **Awkward reality** — the photo captures a completely ordinary situation
  with one deeply awkward detail visible in the background or foreground.
- **Visual contradiction** — the image appears to prove the story while also
  quietly contradicting what a normal person would expect.
- **Bad timing** — the screenshot/photo appears to capture the exact wrong
  moment, creating an unintentionally hilarious result.
- **Overly specific behavior** — a person, animal, object, or account is doing
  something strangely precise that makes the situation funnier.
- **Background joke** — the main subject looks normal, but a secondary detail
  in the background creates a second joke.
- **Human incompetence** — the artifact shows a believable mistake, failed
  attempt, misunderstanding, or poorly thought-out decision.
- **Accidental comedy** — nobody in the artifact appears to realize how funny
  the situation looks.
- **Unnecessary commitment** — someone has gone to an absurd amount of effort
  to solve a very small or mundane problem.
- **Wrong solution** — the person clearly solved the problem, but in a bizarre
  way that creates a new problem.
- **Dead-serious absurdity** — the visual is presented completely normally
  even though the situation itself is ridiculous.
- **Tiny detail with huge implications** — one small visible object, word,
  expression, or environmental detail radically changes how the viewer reads
  the scene.
- **Social awkwardness** — the visual captures a believable moment of
  embarrassment, misunderstanding, confusion, or accidental oversharing.

The visual joke must feel DISCOVERED rather than DESIGNED.

Do not add obvious comedy graphics, reaction stickers, arrows, circles,
captions, meme text, impact fonts, cartoon effects, exaggerated facial
expressions, or other artificial indicators telling the viewer where the joke
is.

The viewer should notice the funny detail naturally.

## 3B. Photographic specificity

When the artifact is a photograph, generate a scene that feels like a real
person happened to capture an unusually funny moment rather than a staged
comedy photograph.

For photo-based artifacts, the frame should be tightly composed and visually
packed with relevant content. Fill the image area with the subject, objects,
and environment; do not leave large empty areas, blank walls, or floating
negative space unless the story specifically requires it. The subject should
feel close to the edges of the frame, with the composition feeling discovered
rather than posed.

Prefer:

- imperfect framing
- slightly inconvenient camera angles
- ordinary locations
- realistic clutter
- believable lighting
- natural human expressions
- people looking away from the camera when appropriate
- objects positioned imperfectly
- mundane backgrounds
- accidental cropping
- small environmental details
- believable physical mistakes
- oddly specific objects
- realistic animal behavior
- situations that look accidental rather than posed
- tight, full-frame compositions that use the entire image area

Avoid:

- cinematic composition
- professional photography
- perfectly centered subjects
- studio lighting
- exaggerated reactions
- obviously AI-generated weirdness
- impossible physics
- surreal objects unless the story specifically requires them
- "random funny stuff" inserted only to make the image humorous
- large empty backgrounds, dead space, or wide negative margins
- any readable text on the photo itself, including signs, labels, stickers,
  product packaging text, phone screens, menus, clothing text, or visible
  writing in the environment

The visual should look like something someone genuinely found, photographed, or
screenshotted and immediately thought, "what am I looking at?" The photo
inside the story must be visually clean and text-free; if a readable word or
label appears, it breaks the illusion and must be removed.

## 3C. Visual punchline hierarchy

When creating a funny artifact, prioritize humor in this order:

1. The situation itself
2. The specific visual detail
3. The written story
4. The reaction/comment

The image must not depend entirely on the caption to become funny.

Whenever possible, make the viewer notice the main situation first, then discover
one additional detail after looking for another second.

The secondary detail should enhance the original joke rather than introduce a
completely separate story.

## 3D. Realistic imperfection

Funny internet discoveries often feel funny because they are imperfect.

Do not make every element look clean, intentional, or professionally arranged.

For photographs, allow believable imperfections such as:

- slightly crooked framing
- clutter
- awkward cropping
- ordinary household objects
- strange but plausible positioning
- mild motion blur when appropriate
- imperfect exposure
- an unflattering but natural camera angle
- people caught mid-action
- animals behaving unpredictably
- objects partially obscuring other objects

For screenshots, allow believable imperfections such as:

- awkward wording
- typos when relevant
- strange capitalization
- inconsistent punctuation
- unnecessarily specific phrasing
- awkward usernames
- mildly chaotic conversation flow

Do NOT add imperfections randomly. Every imperfection must feel native to the
artifact and believable for the platform.

## 3E. The "why did they do that?" test

For comedy-focused generations, try to create one moment where the viewer's
natural reaction is:

"Why would anyone do that?"

The answer should NOT be immediately explained.

The behavior must remain plausible enough that the viewer can imagine a real
person actually doing it.

The funniest situations are often not impossible. They are ordinary human
decisions taken one step further than common sense would recommend.

## 3F. Animal comedy

When animals appear, do not make them behave like cartoon characters.

Use realistic animal behavior combined with human interpretation.

Examples of useful mechanisms:

- an animal appearing to deliberately ignore an obvious situation
- an animal occupying an extremely inconvenient location
- an animal accidentally causing a mundane problem
- an animal appearing completely comfortable while humans are confused
- an animal interacting with an oddly specific household object
- an animal caught at an unfortunate but believable moment

The animal itself should not need to have an exaggerated facial expression.

The humor should come from the situation.

## 3G. No forced visual jokes

Never add a funny object, person, facial expression, background event, sign,
or visual detail unless it has a direct relationship to the story.

Do not create a "busy funny background."

Do not put multiple jokes into one image.

Do not make every person react dramatically.

Do not make every animal look mischievous.

Do not add easter eggs merely because they are funny.

ONE strong visual joke is better than five unrelated jokes.

## 3H. Internet-discovery authenticity

The artifact should feel like it existed BEFORE the story was written.

It should look as though someone encountered it naturally and decided to share
it.

For photographs, prefer the visual language of:

- casual phone photos
- security-camera captures when relevant
- screenshots of someone's original post
- ordinary social-media uploads
- accidental photos
- blurry-but-readable snapshots
- mundane documentation of something unexpectedly strange
- closely cropped, full-frame discoveries with no wasted space

For text artifacts, preserve the feeling of an actual person writing quickly
rather than a professional comedy writer constructing a joke.

For photo-based artifacts, the image must be free of all readable text. No
text on signs, labels, screens, packaging, shirts, posters, notes, or any
other object. If the story requires an in-scene artifact, it should be a clean
image with no written language anywhere within the frame. Keep the composition
tight and occupied so the photo feels intentional without being staged.

The artifact must never look like a purpose-built advertisement, comedy graphic,
or polished content-production asset.

## 4. Specificity rule (content-blind)

Whatever fact, quote, name, or number gets invented — for any emotional
target, not just comedy — it must be concrete and exact, never vague or
round. This applies to every layer: opening claim, artifact contents, and
reaction. Invent the specifics fresh each run, calibrated to the mechanism
chosen in step 2.

## 5. Artifact selection logic

Ask: what would a real person actually have screenshotted to share this?
Let the answer fall out of the emotional target and mechanism, not a fixed
list:

- something visually verifiable → a photo or video frame
- something said or typed → a chat thread, comment section, or post
- something looked up or answered → a search/AI-assistant screenshot
- something involving two states or two subjects → a side-by-side or
  before/after pairing
- something that got buried → an unremarkable-looking post/account that
  undersells its own contents

Only one artifact type should dominate. Stack two only if the mechanism
genuinely needs it (e.g. a claim on one platform verified by evidence on
another).

## 6. Structural layers (use only what the story needs)

- **Opening line** — states the setup in specific terms. Precision creates
  the reaction on its own — it doesn't need to withhold information. Register
  ranges from clean/factual to casually typed depending on platform.
- **Artifact** — the evidence, styled with real UI conventions of its actual
  platform. In-artifact text is fine when the artifact type is inherently
  textual.
- **Artifact relevance rule:** the image or screenshot inside the story must be
  directly relevant to the story's actual subject, event, object, location, or
  reveal. It must not be a random generic stock image, unrelated photo, or
  decorative visual that could belong to any story.

  For comedy-focused generations, the artifact should ideally contain a
  visually observable reason the story is funny. The viewer should be able to
  discover at least one specific visual detail that reinforces, complicates,
  or unexpectedly reframes the written story.

  The visual joke must arise naturally from the documented situation. Never
  manufacture comedy by adding unrelated funny objects, meme imagery,
  exaggerated reactions, stickers, arrows, circles, captions, or other
  editorial comedy devices.

Use the minimal structure the mechanism needs — some posts are just an
escalating thread with no separate artifact or reaction at all.

## 7. Addictiveness / shareability engineering

- **Give it a send-to-a-friend hook.** Somewhere in the post — usually the
  reaction line — there should be a moment specific enough that a reader
  pictures exactly who they'd send it to, without the post saying "tag
  someone" outright.
- **Reward a second look.** Where possible, plant one small detail that only
  lands on a reread (a word choice, a number, a name) rather than putting
  every payoff in the first pass.
- **One clean emotional hit, not several diluted ones.** Resist stacking
  multiple jokes or twists — a single sharp hit outperforms a cluttered one.
- **Vary the shape across generations.** Alternate emotional targets,
  mechanisms, and artifact types run over run so the body of output doesn't
  converge on one recognizable formula.

## 8. Realism discipline

- Every piece of visible text must be complete and specific — never a
  placeholder.
- No design polish: plain white or true dark-mode background, no gradients,
  no stock-photo lighting, no ad-like composition. It should look scrolled
  past and screenshotted, not designed.
- Output must be strictly 3:4 aspect ratio. No landscape, square, or unusual
  framing.
- Every profile picture must be realistic, platform-appropriate, and specific to
  that platform's social context. For example: casual personal portrait for a
  personal account, clean brand-style avatar for a business account, or a
  niche-interest profile image if the platform implies that. The avatar should
  look like a real person or real account, not a cartoon, abstract icon, or
  generic AI face.

## 9. Hard pipeline constraints (non-negotiable, every generation)

These exist because the output feeds an automated OCR + TTS pipeline, so the
frame must contain only the story and the comments — nothing else the OCR
could pick up as noise.

- **Every username and every display name must contain at least one letter and
  at least one digit or an underscore (`_`).** No exceptions, no numeric-only,
  underscore-only, or clean plain-text names.
- **Display names:** if there is a display name, it must include at least one
  letter and at least one digit, with no underscore. Example: `moss9`,
  `jarvis4`, `glow7` are valid. A display name with only digits or with an
  underscore is not allowed.
- **Usernames:** usernames may include `@` only if they also contain at least
  one letter and at least one digit or underscore in the name. Example:
  `@moss9`, `@tide_4`, `@palm7` are valid. Plain names without letters or
  without digits/underscores are not allowed, and `@1234` or `@name` are not
  allowed.
- **No engagement counts anywhere** — no likes, shares, saves, views, reposts,
  replies, follower counts, or any other number that isn't part of the story
  itself.
- **No dates or timestamps anywhere** — no post time, no "time ago" labels,
  no date stamps. The frame should contain only the caption/story text and
  the comment(s), nothing else.
- **Strict output ratio: 3:4.** The final render must be portrait and must not
  drift from the 3:4 aspect ratio.
- **All profile pictures must be realistic and platform-appropriate.** They must
  match the social context and feel like real account avatars, not generic AI
  portraits, memes, or abstract icons.
- **No famous meme imagery** may be used as the central artifact or visual
  evidence.

## 10. Before finalizing, check

- Did I pick a single emotional target and let it drive every choice?
- Is the story weird, funny, shocking, exciting, and energetic enough to match
  a lively spoken TTS delivery?
- Does the story have a sharp, darkly funny, unsettling, or shocking edge?
- Is the reaction line free of any explanation of its own joke?
- Do the comments carry a dark, sarcastic, dry, or wry tone rather than
  bland or upbeat reactions?
- Is every fact/number/name specific rather than generic?
- Does the artifact match what a real person would actually screenshot?
- Is the picture inside the story directly relevant to the specific event,
  object, or scene being described?
- Is the photo completely free of readable text, labels, signs, stickers,
  writing, or screen text?
- Is the composition tight and filled with subject matter, with no large empty
  space or dead margins?
- Is there a moment someone would send to a specific friend?
- Does this differ in shape/emotion from the last few generations?
- Does every username/display name include at least one letter and a digit or underscore?
- If a display name exists, does it include a letter + digit and no underscore?
- If a username contains @, does it still include a letter and a digit or underscore?
- Is the frame completely free of counts, dates, and timestamps?
- Is the final output locked to a strict 3:4 portrait ratio?
- Are all profile pictures realistic, platform-appropriate, and non-generic?
- Is the main artifact original and not a famous meme or recognizable meme frame?
- If the target is funny/absurd, does the IMAGE itself contain a genuine
  visual-comedy mechanism rather than merely illustrating the text?
- Is there exactly one primary visual joke?
- Does the visual joke arise naturally from the specific situation?
- Would the artifact still be interesting or amusing if the caption were
  temporarily hidden?
- Is the funniest detail discoverable rather than explicitly pointed out?
- Does the image feel accidentally captured rather than professionally staged?
- Are imperfections believable and native to the artifact?
- Is there any unrelated "funny background" detail that should be removed?
- Are facial expressions and animal behavior realistic rather than cartoonish?
- Does the image create a natural "why did they do that?" reaction?
- Does the artifact look like something that genuinely existed before someone
  decided to share it?

Invent the emotional target, mechanism, subject, artifact, accounts, and any
reaction text fresh each time this framework is run.
