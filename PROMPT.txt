# "Found This On The Internet" Post — Idea-Engineering Framework

This is a reusable engine, not a template with content in it. Every run
invents its own fact, artifact, accounts, and comment from scratch. Nothing
below should be copied into an output — it's the machine that builds it.

## 1. Pick the emotional target first

Before anything else, pick the ONE feeling this post should leave someone
with. This choice drives every downstream decision — mechanism, voice,
artifact, pacing. Rotate across these; don't default to the same one every
time, or the output starts feeling like a template instead of a real find:

- **Funny / absurd** — the reader laughs or sends it to someone immediately.
- **Petty outrage / relatable exasperation** — "this is so real it's annoying."
- **Uncanny / unsettling** — something quietly wrong that lingers.
- **Jaw-dropping disbelief** — "there's no way this is real."
- **Wholesome subversion** — braces for something bad, gets something sweet
  (or the reverse — braces for wholesome, gets a dark turn).

A post that's merely "mildly interesting" won't move. Pick a target strong
enough that someone would tag a friend or reply within three seconds of
reading it.

The story itself should be weird, funny, shocking, chaotic, lively, and
exciting. Boring, flat, or slowly-building stories are not allowed. The core
story should feel like a real person is urgently telling the most unbelievable
thing they saw, heard, or found online, with a strong sense of momentum and
surprise.

Favor stories that feel genuinely alarming, uncanny, darkly funny, or deeply
strange. The best versions hit with a sharp mix of disbelief, dread, and
comic timing. The story should not be safe or polite; it should feel like the
kind of thing that makes someone stop scrolling and immediately send it to a
friend.

Allowed tone includes dry, suggestive, teasing innuendo and playful sexual
humor when it is indirect, not explicit, and not graphic. Keep it implied,
clever, and wink-and-nudge rather than explicit or pornographic.

## 2. The underlying mechanism

Every version of this format runs on an asymmetry, stated plainly, then
reframed. Pick ONE, chosen to serve the emotional target from step 1:

- **Magnitude mismatch** — a small input maps to a wildly disproportionate
  output, or something that sounds huge turns out nearly invisible.
- **Escalation** — a sequence that starts ordinary and, step by step, tips
  into absurd. The reveal is the trajectory, not one fact.
- **Claim vs. artifact** — an unbelievable statement, followed by something
  that looks like proof.
- **Deadpan reframe** — something that reads as neutral until a second party
  points out its funny or uncomfortable implication.
- **Mundane container, extraordinary content** — boring packaging undersells
  something huge inside it.

## 3. Comedy craft (apply whenever the target is funny/absurd/outrage)

Humor has its own mechanics, separate from surprise. When comedy is the
target, follow these or the "joke" will just read as a fact with a laugh
emoji attached:

- **Never explain the joke.** The reaction/comment should never state what's
  funny about the setup — it should just add a new, deadpan detail that makes
  the reader do the connecting themselves. Explaining kills it every time.
- **Specificity is the punchline.** An oddly precise, slightly unnecessary
  detail is inherently funnier than a vague one. When inventing content,
  always reach for the more specific, weirder-but-plausible version of any
  number, name, or noun.
- **Understatement beats escalation in the punchline.** The setup can
  escalate; the reaction/final line almost never should — a flat, dry final
  beat lands harder than an exclamation point.
- **Dark humor is allowed when it fits the story.** A comment can land with a
  dry, darkly funny undercut, a sense of dread, or a bitter little twist as
  long as it still feels human and not mean-spirited or forced.
- **Incongruity, not absurdity for its own sake.** The funniest pairing is
  two believable things that shouldn't belong in the same sentence, not one
  outlandish invented thing.
- **Let the reader be the one who "gets it."** If the joke requires the post
  to point at itself, cut it and find a version that doesn't.

## 4. Specificity rule (content-blind)

Whatever fact, quote, name, or number gets invented — for any emotional
target, not just comedy — it must be concrete and exact, never vague or
round. This applies to every layer: opening claim, artifact contents, and
reaction. Invent the specifics fresh each run, calibrated to the mechanism
chosen in step 2.

## 5. Artifact selection logic

Ask: what would a real person actually have screenshotted to share this?
Let the answer fall out of the emotional target and mechanism, not a fixed
list:

- something visually verifiable → a photo or video frame
- something said or typed → a chat thread, comment section, or post
- something looked up or answered → a search/AI-assistant screenshot
- something involving two states or two subjects → a side-by-side or
  before/after pairing
- something that got buried → an unremarkable-looking post/account that
  undersells its own contents

Only one artifact type should dominate. Stack two only if the mechanism
genuinely needs it (e.g. a claim on one platform verified by evidence on
another).

## 6. Structural layers (use only what the story needs)

- **Opening line** — states the setup in specific terms. Precision creates
  the reaction on its own — it doesn't need to withhold information. Register
  ranges from clean/factual to casually typed depending on platform.
- **Artifact** — the evidence, styled with real UI conventions of its actual
  platform. In-artifact text is fine when the artifact type is inherently
  textual.
- **Artifact relevance rule:** the image or screenshot inside the story must be
  directly relevant to the story's actual subject, event, object, location, or
  reveal. It must not be a random generic stock image, unrelated photo, or
  decorative visual that could belong to any story. The picture should look
  like a real, story-specific artifact that a person would genuinely have
  captured and shared.
- **No famous meme imagery:** do not use recognizable meme templates, famous
  meme frames, viral meme stills, or iconic internet meme screenshots as the
  core visual evidence. The artifact must be original, obscure, and specific
  to the story, not a famous internet meme.
- **Reaction** — optional. Include only when a second voice adds a genuinely
  new angle (widens the implication, undercuts it, or notices something about
  the reaction itself) rather than restating the opening line.

Use the minimal structure the mechanism needs — some posts are just an
escalating thread with no separate artifact or reaction at all.

## 7. Addictiveness / shareability engineering

- **Give it a send-to-a-friend hook.** Somewhere in the post — usually the
  reaction line — there should be a moment specific enough that a reader
  pictures exactly who they'd send it to, without the post saying "tag
  someone" outright.
- **Reward a second look.** Where possible, plant one small detail that only
  lands on a reread (a word choice, a number, a name) rather than putting
  every payoff in the first pass.
- **One clean emotional hit, not several diluted ones.** Resist stacking
  multiple jokes or twists — a single sharp hit outperforms a cluttered one.
- **Vary the shape across generations.** Alternate emotional targets,
  mechanisms, and artifact types run over run so the body of output doesn't
  converge on one recognizable formula.

## 8. Realism discipline

- Every piece of visible text must be complete and specific — never a
  placeholder.
- No design polish: plain white or true dark-mode background, no gradients,
  no stock-photo lighting, no ad-like composition. It should look scrolled
  past and screenshotted, not designed.
- Output must be strictly 3:4 aspect ratio. No landscape, square, or unusual
  framing.
- Every profile picture must be realistic, platform-appropriate, and specific to
  that platform's social context. For example: casual personal portrait for a
  personal account, clean brand-style avatar for a business account, or a
  niche-interest profile image if the platform implies that. The avatar should
  look like a real person or real account, not a cartoon, abstract icon, or
  generic AI face.

## 9. Hard pipeline constraints (non-negotiable, every generation)

These exist because the output feeds an automated OCR + TTS pipeline, so the
frame must contain only the story and the comments — nothing else the OCR
could pick up as noise.

- **Every username and every display name must contain at least one digit or
  an underscore (`_`).** No exceptions, no clean plain-text names.
- **Display names:** if there is a display name, it must contain digits only,
  with no underscore. Example: `moss9`, `jarvis4`, `glow7` is valid. A display
  name with an underscore is not allowed.
- **Usernames:** usernames may include `@` only if they also contain a digit or
  underscore in the name. Example: `@moss9`, `@tide_4`, `@palm7` are valid.
  Plain names without numbers or underscores are not allowed, and `@name`
  without a digit or underscore is not allowed.
- **No engagement counts anywhere** — no likes, shares, saves, views, reposts,
  replies, follower counts, or any other number that isn't part of the story
  itself.
- **No dates or timestamps anywhere** — no post time, no "time ago" labels,
  no date stamps. The frame should contain only the caption/story text and
  the comment(s), nothing else.
- **Strict output ratio: 3:4.** The final render must be portrait and must not
  drift from the 3:4 aspect ratio.
- **All profile pictures must be realistic and platform-appropriate.** They must
  match the social context and feel like real account avatars, not generic AI
  portraits, memes, or abstract icons.
- **No famous meme imagery** may be used as the central artifact or visual
  evidence.

## 10. Before finalizing, check

- Did I pick a single emotional target and let it drive every choice?
- Is the story weird, funny, shocking, exciting, and energetic enough to match
  a lively spoken TTS delivery?
- Does the story have a sharp, darkly funny, unsettling, or shocking edge?
- Is the reaction line free of any explanation of its own joke?
- Is every fact/number/name specific rather than generic?
- Does the artifact match what a real person would actually screenshot?
- Is the picture inside the story directly relevant to the specific event,
  object, or scene being described?
- Is there a moment someone would send to a specific friend?
- Does this differ in shape/emotion from the last few generations?
- Does every username/display name have a digit or underscore?
- If a display name exists, does it use digits only and no underscore?
- If a username contains @, does it still contain a digit or underscore?
- Is the frame completely free of counts, dates, and timestamps?
- Is the final output locked to a strict 3:4 portrait ratio?
- Are all profile pictures realistic, platform-appropriate, and non-generic?
- Is the main artifact original and not a famous meme or recognizable meme frame?

Invent the emotional target, mechanism, subject, artifact, accounts, and any
reaction text fresh each time this framework is run.
